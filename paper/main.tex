\begin{abstract}
A $k\times n$ Latin rectangle is \emph{balanced} when every pair of symbols lies, summed over
the rows, at the same total distance; the \emph{imbalance} measures how far a rectangle is from
this ideal. Minimum-imbalance Latin rectangles are used in the design of agronomic field
experiments, and finding them has become a benchmark for constraint programming, SAT, and,
most recently, LLM-generated streamliners combined with mixed-integer programming. We show
that a plain simulated-annealing search with incremental cost updates and an adaptive penalty
for column conflicts improves the best known imbalance on \NumImproved{} of the \NumCells{}
non-square instances with $8\le n\le 13$ studied in the literature, and matches the previous
value on the remaining \NumMatched{}. The improvements average \MeanGain\% and reach
\MaxGain\% (\TopCell: \TopOld{} $\to$ \TopNew). A single one-minute run on one laptop core
already beats the previous record on each of these instances, whereas the previous records came
from hours of MIP solving per instance. All rectangles, an independent checker, and the search code are public. The
results are upper bounds: optimality remains open for all improved instances.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Latin squares and rectangles are classical tools of experimental design. In agronomic field
trials, treatments laid out in a Latin rectangle can still be biased by spatial
autocorrelation when some pairs of treatments are systematically planted closer together than
others~\citep{vanEs1993}. \emph{Spatially balanced} Latin squares, in which every pair of
symbols has the same total distance, remove this bias~\citep{GomesSellmann2004,LeBrasGomesSelman2012}.
\citet{DiazLeBrasGomes2017} extended the notion to $k\times n$ rectangles. Balanced rectangles
exist only in restricted cases, so they defined the \emph{imbalance} of a rectangle and asked
for rectangles of minimum imbalance. They found this a hard optimization problem for CSP,
local-search and MIP methods, and proposed it as a benchmark.

Since then the benchmark has been attacked with SAT encodings that also certify optimality via
DRAT proofs~\citep{RamaswamySzeider2023}, and with streamliners generated by large language
models and evaluated with Gurobi~\citep{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}, which improved the best
known values on most open instances. Balanced Latin rectangles also serve as a test problem for
LLM agents that reformulate constraint models~\citep{VoborilSzeider2026}.

\paragraph{Contribution.} We revisit the problem with a deliberately simple method: simulated
annealing~\citep{KirkpatrickGelattVecchi1983} over rows that are permutations, a swap move
evaluated in $O(n)$ time, and a penalty for repeated symbols in a column whose weight adapts
during the search, in the spirit of strategic oscillation~\citep{GloverHao2011}. None of these
ingredients is new. The point of this note is the outcome: on the instances of
\citet{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}, the method improves \NumImproved{} of \NumCells{} best known
values and is never worse, using minutes of laptop time instead of hours of server time per
instance. We release every rectangle together with an independent checker, so each record can
be confirmed in seconds without trusting our solver.

We see two consequences. First, the previous upper bounds on this benchmark were much further
from optimal than they appeared, which matters to anyone using them to judge new methods.
Second, methods that aim at good solutions to this problem, including LLM-based ones, should be
compared against a well-engineered local search and not only against an exact solver run under
a time limit.

\section{Problem}

A $k\times n$ Latin rectangle ($k\le n$) is a $k\times n$ array over the symbols
$\{1,\dots,n\}$ in which no symbol repeats in a row or in a column. Write $c_i(u)$ for the
column of symbol $u$ in row $i$. Following \citet{RamaswamySzeider2023}, the distance of two
symbols and the imbalance of $L$ are
\[
  d(u,v) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} |c_i(u)-c_i(v)|, \qquad
  I(L) = \sum_{1\le u<v\le n} \Bigl| d(u,v) - \tfrac{k(n+1)}{3} \Bigr| .
\]
The target $k(n+1)/3$ is the average of $d(u,v)$ over all pairs, since every row contributes
$\sum_{u<v}|c_i(u)-c_i(v)| = (n+1)n(n-1)/6$. Because $3I(L)$ is an integer, values are written
$x.0$, $x.3$, $x.6$ for $x$, $x+\tfrac13$, $x+\tfrac23$, as in the literature. The goal is a
rectangle of minimum imbalance for given $k$ and $n$.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Search space and move.} The state is $k$ permutations of the symbols, one per row.
The first row is fixed to the identity, which loses no generality because relabelling the
symbols does not change the imbalance. A move picks a row $i\ge 2$ and two columns $j_1\ne j_2$
and swaps the symbols $a=L[i][j_1]$ and $b=L[i][j_2]$. Rows stay permutations, but the swap may
create a repeated symbol in a column.

\paragraph{Incremental evaluation.} We keep the matrix $D$ of all pair distances. A swap in row
$i$ moves only $a$ and $b$, so only the $2(n-2)$ distances $d(a,x)$ and $d(b,x)$ with
$x\notin\{a,b\}$ change, and $d(a,b)$ does not change. For each such $x$, with $p_x=c_i(x)$,
\[
  d'(a,x) = d(a,x) - |j_1-p_x| + |j_2-p_x|, \qquad d'(b,x) = d(b,x) - |j_2-p_x| + |j_1-p_x|,
\]
so the change of $3I(L)$ is computed exactly in $O(n)$ integer operations, against $O(kn^2)$
for recomputing the objective from scratch.

\paragraph{Column conflicts.} Let $V$ be the number of excess symbol occurrences over all
columns, kept by per-column counters and updated in $O(1)$ per move. The search minimizes
$3I(L) + W\cdot V$ and only records rectangles with $V=0$. The weight $W$ starts at $k$; every
$2^{16}$ moves it is multiplied by $1.02$ if the current state has conflicts and by $0.99$
otherwise, clamped to $[1, 4n^2]$. This matters most when $k$ is close to $n$: there, almost
every swap creates a conflict, so a search that effectively forbids conflicts stalls. In a first
version with a large fixed penalty ($W=4n^2$), 10-second runs on $6\times7$ and $7\times7$ never
visited a conflict-free rectangle other than the starting one. With the adaptive weight, 20-second
runs reach the known optima of both (20.0 and 18.6).

\paragraph{Annealing schedule.} The search starts from the cyclic rectangle $L[i][j]=i+j \bmod n$.
A move with change $\Delta$ is accepted if $\Delta\le 0$, and otherwise with probability
$e^{-\Delta/T}$. The temperature starts at $T_0=2k$, is multiplied by $0.9999995$ after every
move, and is kept above $0.05$; it is reset to $T_0$ when the best rectangle has not improved for
$4\cdot 10^{7}$ moves. Each run is single-threaded, stops after a fixed CPU time, and re-checks
its best rectangle from scratch before reporting it. The implementation is about 130 lines of C.

\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Setup.} We use the instances of Table~1 of \citet{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}:
$8\le n\le 13$ and $2\le k\le n-1$, giving \NumCells{} cells. We omit squares ($k=n$), where
the problem is governed by different structure~\citep{XiaEtAl2026}. The baseline in each cell is
the best value reported there, which already includes earlier results and four-hour Gurobi
runs. All runs were done on an Apple M3 laptop (8 cores, 16\,GB), eight single-threaded runs at
a time. We ran one run per cell with a budget of 1 minute, one with 5 minutes (different seed),
and one with 10 minutes for the larger cells ($k\ge4$, $n\ge9$). We report the best rectangle per
cell, so at most 16 CPU-minutes were spent on any instance.

\paragraph{Verification.} Every reported rectangle is checked by an independent Python program
written from the definition above, which uses exact rational arithmetic and shares no code with
the solver. The checker reproduces the imbalance of the three worked examples printed
in~\citep{RamaswamySzeider2023,VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025} ($6$, $4$ and $84$).

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:results} and Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} show the outcome. The
search improves \NumImproved{} cells, matches \NumMatched{}, and is worse on \NumWorse. All
improved cells have $n\ge 9$ and $k\ge 3$. The matched cells are $k\in\{2,3\}$ and $n=8$, several
of which were already proven optimal~\citep{RamaswamySzeider2023}, together with $4\times10$,
$5\times10$ and $6\times12$. Gains grow with the size of the instance (Figure~\ref{fig:dumbbell}),
up to \MaxGain\% for \TopCell. The one-minute runs alone already beat the previous record in
\NumImprovedOneMin{} cells; longer runs mainly widen the margin (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}).

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Why does a simple method do so well?} We have not tested this, but the structure of
the problem suggests an explanation. The objective is a sum of $\binom n2$ absolute deviations. A
MIP model must linearize every one of them, and its LP relaxation can make all pairs look nearly
balanced at once, so it likely gives the solver little guidance. Streamliners help the solver by
restricting it to a structured subspace, but the best rectangles need not lie in that subspace.
Local search has neither problem: the objective is cheap to update exactly, the swap
neighbourhood is natural, and the penalty lets the search cross infeasible states. The setting
suits annealing unusually well, and we do not claim that the comparison carries over to other
problems.

\paragraph{Compute.} The previous records came from about four hours of Gurobi per instance as a
baseline, followed by several hours of streamliner generation and evaluation on 10-core
servers~\citep{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}. Our records use at most 16 CPU-minutes per instance
on a laptop. We did not rerun the MIP pipeline on our hardware, so the comparison is between
reported results, not a controlled experiment.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The new values are upper bounds. We prove nothing about optimality, and
the size of the improvements suggests that the remaining gap to the optimum may still be large.
We ran one search per cell and budget, so we report no variance across seeds. Finally, the
benchmark stops at $n=13$; larger instances are an obvious next step.

\paragraph{How this work was done.} The solver, the checker and this paper were produced in an
interactive session with an AI coding assistant (Claude, by Anthropic), directed by the author.
The assistant selected the problem from a survey of recent papers, wrote and debugged the
code, and drafted the text; the author chose the overall direction and reviewed the results. We
mention this for transparency, not as a contribution: the method is standard, and we have not
tested whether the result depends on the assistant.

\section{Conclusion}

A short, standard simulated-annealing search improves \NumImproved{} of the \NumCells{}
best-known imbalance values for balanced Latin rectangles studied in the recent literature, and
matches the rest. Natural next steps are lower bounds or optimality proofs for the improved
cells, for instance with the SAT approach of \citet{RamaswamySzeider2023}, larger instances, and
controlled comparisons with exact and LLM-guided methods on equal hardware.
