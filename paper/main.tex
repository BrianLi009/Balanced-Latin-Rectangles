\begin{abstract}
A $k\times n$ Latin rectangle is balanced when every pair of symbols has the same total
distance, summed over the rows. We evaluate simulated annealing with an $O(n)$ within-row
swap and an adaptive penalty for column conflicts. Against Table~1 of the CP 2025 study,
it improves \NumImproved{} of \NumCells{} non-square instances with $8\le n\le13$ and
matches the other \NumMatched{}. The reduction averages \MeanGain\% over the improved
instances and reaches \MaxGain\% (\TopCell: \TopOld{} $\to$ \TopNew).
These final values use up to 16 allocated CPU minutes per instance across separate runs.
In a separate first-improvement experiment on the \NumImproved{} improved instances,
all \NumTimingRuns{} runs beat the reference bound: the pooled median is \MedianBeat{}
CPU seconds and the maximum is \MaxBeat{} seconds. Four older SAT targets are reached
or improved within \MaxMatch{} seconds in all \NumSeeds{} runs per target.
These historical timing measurements are not controlled speedup comparisons: hardware
differs, and CP 2025 reports budgets rather than time to the reference quality.
We provide the rectangles, an independent exact checker, and code. The improved values
are feasible upper bounds, not optimality certificates.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Latin squares and rectangles are classical tools of experimental design. In a field trial,
spatial autocorrelation can bias a Latin layout when some pairs of treatments are always planted
closer together than others~\citep{vanEs1993}. \emph{Spatially balanced} Latin squares address this source of spatial imbalance, because every pair of symbols has the same total
distance~\citep{GomesSellmann2004,LeBrasGomesSelman2012}. \citet{DiazLeBrasGomes2017} extended
the idea to $k\times n$ rectangles. Balanced rectangles exist only in restricted cases. Díaz et
al.\ therefore defined the \emph{imbalance} of a rectangle and asked for rectangles of minimum
imbalance. They compared CSP, local search and MIP, and reported that local search outperformed MIP
on larger instances with $k>3$. Their local search swaps whole columns and samples row
subsets of cyclic squares. Our within-row moves explore a different space and may cross
column-infeasible states; we do not claim to introduce local search for this problem.

Two recent studies attacked this benchmark. \citet{RamaswamySzeider2023} used SAT encodings,
which also certify optimality with DRAT proofs. \citet{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025} used
streamliners generated by large language models (LLMs) and evaluated them with Gurobi. This
approach improved the best known values on most open instances. Balanced Latin rectangles also
serve as a test problem for LLM agents that reformulate constraint models~\citep{VoborilSzeider2026}.

\paragraph{Contribution.} We revisit the problem with a deliberately simple method. It is
simulated annealing~\citep{KirkpatrickGelattVecchi1983} over rows that are permutations, with a
swap move that costs $O(n)$ time to evaluate. A penalty for repeated symbols in a column adapts
its weight during the search, in the spirit of strategic oscillation~\citep{GloverHao2011}. None
of these ingredients is new. Our contribution is a set of independently verified improved
upper bounds. Throughout, \emph{record} means the reference value in Table~1 of
\citet{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}; it does not assert priority over every later or
unpublished result. The search improves \NumImproved{} of these \NumCells{} values and
matches the rest. The timing experiment concerns first improvement over the reference,
not the time to obtain the final values. These results motivate including a strong
problem-specific local-search baseline alongside exact and LLM-guided methods.

\section{Problem}

A $k\times n$ Latin rectangle ($k\le n$) is a $k\times n$ array over the symbols
$\{1,\dots,n\}$ in which no symbol repeats in a row or in a column. Write $c_i(u)$ for the
column of symbol $u$ in row $i$. Following \citet{RamaswamySzeider2023}, the distance of two
symbols and the imbalance of $L$ are
\[
  d(u,v) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} |c_i(u)-c_i(v)|, \qquad
  I(L) = \sum_{1\le u<v\le n} \Bigl| d(u,v) - \tfrac{k(n+1)}{3} \Bigr| .
\]
The target $k(n+1)/3$ is the average of $d(u,v)$ over all pairs, because every row contributes
$\sum_{u<v}|c_i(u)-c_i(v)| = (n+1)n(n-1)/6$. The value $3I(L)$ is always an integer. As in the
literature, we write imbalances as $x.0$, $x.3$ and $x.6$ for $x$, $x+\tfrac13$ and
$x+\tfrac23$. The goal is a rectangle of minimum imbalance for given $k$ and $n$.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Search space and move.} The state is $k$ permutations of the symbols, one per row. We
fix the first row to the identity. This loses no generality, because a relabelling of the
symbols does not change the imbalance. A move picks a row $i\ge 2$ and two columns
$j_1\ne j_2$, and it swaps the symbols $a=L[i][j_1]$ and $b=L[i][j_2]$. Rows stay permutations,
but the swap can create a repeated symbol in a column.

\paragraph{Incremental evaluation.} We keep the matrix $D$ of all pair distances. A swap in row
$i$ moves only $a$ and $b$. So only the $2(n-2)$ distances $d(a,x)$ and $d(b,x)$ with
$x\notin\{a,b\}$ change, and $d(a,b)$ stays the same. For each such $x$, with $p_x=c_i(x)$,
\[
  d'(a,x) = d(a,x) - |j_1-p_x| + |j_2-p_x|, \qquad d'(b,x) = d(b,x) - |j_2-p_x| + |j_1-p_x|.
\]
The search therefore computes the exact change of $3I(L)$ in $O(n)$ integer operations. A
recomputation from scratch costs $O(kn^2)$.

\paragraph{Column conflicts.} Let $V$ be the number of excess symbol occurrences over all
columns. Per-column counters give $V$ in $O(1)$ time per move. The search minimizes
$3I(L) + W\cdot V$, and it records only rectangles with $V=0$. The weight $W$ starts at $k$.
Every $2^{16}$ proposals, including proposals with identical columns, the search multiplies
$W$ by $1.02$ if the current state has conflicts and by $0.99$ otherwise, clamping it to
$[1,4n^2]$. This allows exploration through infeasible states while retaining only feasible
incumbents. We have not isolated the benefit of this adaptation in a controlled ablation.

\paragraph{Annealing schedule.} The search starts from the cyclic rectangle
$L[i][j]=i+j \bmod n$. It accepts a move with change $\Delta$ if $\Delta\le 0$, and otherwise
with probability $e^{-\Delta/T}$. The temperature starts at $T_0=2k$ and is multiplied by
$0.9999995$ after each nontrivial proposal, accepted or rejected, with a floor of $0.05$.
The implementation samples columns independently and skips proposals with $j_1=j_2$.
At checks every $2^{16}$ proposals, it reheats after more than $4\cdot10^7$ proposals
since an improvement or the last reheat. Reheating also resets this counter.
Each run is single-threaded. Its CPU timer starts after initialization and excludes
process launch, output and external verification. Budget checks can overshoot by a
check interval. Before reporting the best rectangle, the compact C implementation
recomputes its imbalance from scratch.

\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Setup.} We use the instances in Table~1 of \citet{VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}, with
$8\le n\le 13$ and $2\le k\le n-1$. This gives \NumCells{} instances. We omit squares ($k=n$),
because different structure governs that case~\citep{XiaEtAl2026}. The record of each instance is
the best value reported there. It already includes earlier results and four-hour Gurobi runs. We
used an Apple M3 laptop (8 cores, 16\,GB) and ran eight single-threaded runs at a time. Each
instance got one run with a budget of 1 minute and one run with 5 minutes, with different seeds.
The larger instances ($k\ge4$, $n\ge9$) also got one 10-minute run. We report the best rectangle
per instance, so the largest allocated total is 16 CPU minutes per instance. The 137 runs
have a total allocated budget of 10 hours 56 minutes; these are CPU budgets, not elapsed
wall times. The timing CSV preserves the original measurements. Their per-run stdout,
stderr and build provenance were not archived, so they cannot be reconstructed from
that CSV alone; the revised timing runner archives these for future experiments.

\paragraph{Verification.} An independent Python program checks every reported rectangle. We wrote
it from the definition above. It uses exact rational arithmetic and shares no code with the
solver. As a finite cross-check of the published quantity, we ran it on all fourteen
rectangles printed in~\citep{RamaswamySzeider2023,VoborilRamaswamySzeider2025}. These include the
eight CP 2025 records of their Figure~6, up to $12\times13$. The checker reproduces every reported
imbalance exactly.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:results} shows the outcome. The
search improves \NumImproved{} instances, matches \NumMatched{}, and is worse on \NumWorse. All
improved instances have $n\ge 9$ and $k\ge 3$. The search matches the record on all instances
with $n=8$ or $k=2$, on all instances with $k=3$ except $3\times12$, and on $4\times10$,
$5\times10$ and $6\times12$. Several of these records are proven
optimal~\citep{RamaswamySzeider2023}. The maximum reduction is \MaxGain\% for \TopCell;
the mean over the \NumImproved{} improved instances is \MeanGain\%.
The one-minute runs alone improve \NumImprovedOneMin{} instances. Over the
\NumBudgetCells{} instances with all three budgets, mean reductions are \BudgetOneMin\%,
\BudgetFiveMin\% and \BudgetTenMin\% for the separate one-, five- and ten-minute runs.
Because each budget uses a different seed, these are not checkpoints of one trajectory
or an isolated estimate of the effect of time. Individual outcomes need not improve
monotonically with budget.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Incremental evaluation makes each proposal inexpensive, while
penalized column conflicts allow transitions between feasible rectangles. Weak LP
relaxations and restrictions imposed by streamliners are possible explanations for the
relative results, but we did not measure relaxation gaps or isolate these mechanisms.
The experiments establish achievable bounds, not a causal explanation of performance.

\paragraph{Relation to earlier local search.} Local search for balanced Latin designs
predates this work~\citep{SmithGomesFernandez2005,DiazLeBrasGomes2017}.
In particular, the 2017 rectangle study already found local search competitive with,
and on larger cases better than, MIP. Its whole-column swaps and selection of rows from
cyclic squares differ from our within-row neighborhood and infeasible intermediate
states. We have not rerun that method head-to-head, so we cannot attribute our gains
to one of these differences. The newer bounds provide a useful reference for exact,
LLM-guided and local-search approaches; they do not establish a general ranking of
these algorithm families.

\paragraph{Timing comparability.} The original measurements used an Apple M3 laptop;
the earlier studies used Intel Xeon E5-2640~v4 servers. We did not calibrate their
relative performance or rerun the earlier pipelines. CP 2025 does not report time to
each reference bound. CP 2023 reports finding times for satisfiable decision instances,
but its solver also supports infeasibility proofs, which our heuristic cannot provide.
Initialization, launch and verification are outside our reported search timer.
Consequently neither comparison establishes a hardware-controlled speedup.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The new values are upper bounds. We prove nothing about optimality, and
the size of the improvements suggests that the remaining gap to the optimum may still be large.
The final records come from one run per instance and budget. Only the speed experiment uses
several seeds. We also lack controlled penalty and neighborhood ablations and a direct comparison
with the earlier local-search implementation. Finally, the benchmark stops at $n=13$, and larger instances are an obvious next
step.

\section{Conclusion}

A short, standard simulated-annealing search improves \NumImproved{} of the \NumCells{} reference
imbalance values for balanced Latin rectangles in CP 2025 Table~1, and it matches
the rest. All 165 first-improvement runs on the 33 improved instances beat their reference
within 143 CPU seconds in the historical timing data. This is distinct from the
final-quality experiment and does not establish a controlled speedup. Natural next steps follow from this.
\begin{itemize}
\item Prove lower bounds or optimality for the improved instances, for example with the SAT
approach of \citet{RamaswamySzeider2023}.
\item Extend the benchmark to larger instances.
\item Compare exact, LLM-guided and local-search methods on equal hardware.
\end{itemize}
